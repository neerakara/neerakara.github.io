Self-Supervised Representation Learning in Vision

Learning
Notes on contrastive learning, self-distillation, and masked prediction.

A visual encoder learns to represent whatever its training objective rewards. Supervised training optimizes for a fixed label set: the resulting representations encode what distinguishes those labels, which may not be what a different task requires. Self-supervised learning (SSL) sidesteps labels entirely, learning representations directly from the structure of images. This note covers the three main SSL principles: contrastive learning, self-distillation, and masked prediction.

NoteMotivation

Supervised pretraining on large labelled datasets, followed by fine-tuning, works well when the target task resembles the pretraining labels. However, it has two structural limitations: (1) Labels are expensive. In domains like medical imaging, curating a large labelled dataset requires significant expert effort, while unlabelled data is far more accessible. (2), labels impose a particular partitioning of the visual world. A classifier trained on ImageNet learns representations shaped by ImageNet categories; those features may not be the most relevant for a different task. SSL optimizes no specific label set; it learns the general structure of the visual world.

SSL became the dominant pretraining paradigm for three reasons: (1) unlabelled data are abundant; (2) ViTs, lacking the inductive biases of CNNs, scale particularly well on diverse unlabelled data; and (3) the results are compelling. DINOv2 and DINOv3, trained on hundreds of millions of images, produce encoders that support diverse downstream tasks from a single frozen backbone.

NoteWhat Is a Good Representation?

A representation is a function \(f_\theta\) that maps an image \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}\) to a feature vector \(z \in \mathbb{R}^d\):

\[ \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}}{x} \;\xrightarrow{\;f_\theta\;}\; \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}\mathbb{R}^d}{z} \]

A good \(z\) should capture the underlying factors of variation in an image (identity, pose, disease state, lighting) while discarding nuisance surface variation, as formalized in a seminal framework for representation learning. This motivates four concrete properties.

Semantic. Images with similar content should map to nearby points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). A chest X-ray with pneumonia should be closer to other pneumonia cases than to normal scans.

Invariant. The representation should be insensitive to changes that do not affect semantics: lighting, viewpoint, color, slight cropping. These are nuisance factors: they change pixels but not meaning.

Discriminative. Images with different semantic content should be far apart. Invariance alone is not enough; the trivial solution of mapping everything to zero is perfectly invariant but useless.

Transferable. The features should be reusable across tasks without being retrained from scratch. This favors general structure over task-specific detail.

These four properties define the target. The three SSL methods in Section 4 each pursue them from a different angle.

NoteThe Self-Supervised Setup

The core insight behind visual SSL is that a single image can be transformed in many ways that preserve its semantic content. A crop of a dog is still a dog. A color-jittered chest X-ray still shows the same anatomy.

Given an image \(x\), we sample two augmented views by drawing two stochastic transformations \(t_1, t_2\) from an augmentation distribution \(\mathcal{T}\):

\[ x_1 = t_1(x), \qquad x_2 = t_2(x), \qquad t_1, t_2 \sim \mathcal{T} \]

Each \(t_i\) applies a random combination of operations such as random resized crop, horizontal flip, color jitter, grayscale, and Gaussian blur. We call \((x_1, x_2)\) a positive pair: same semantic content, different appearance. The learning signal comes entirely from this structure: a good encoder should map \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) to nearby representations. Images from different sources are negatives; their representations should be far apart.

The choice of augmentations defines what the encoder learns to be invariant to; random resized crops with large scale variation are the most critical choice, forcing the encoder to recognize content across different spatial extents rather than memorizing texture.

NoteThree Ways to Learn Without Labels

Given two semantically equivalent views \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), how should we define the learning objective? There are three dominant answers.

NoteA. Contrastive Learning

Idea. Compare against negatives: pull positive pairs together, push negatives apart.

Each view passes through the encoder \(f_\theta\) and a small projection head, producing a normalized embedding \(z_i\):

\[ \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}}{x_i} \;\xrightarrow{\;f_\theta\;}\; \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}\mathbb{R}^d}{h_i} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{project + norm}\;}\; \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}\|\cdot\|=1}{z_i} \]

The projection head is discarded after pretraining; \(h_i\) is the representation used downstream. The loss treats matching as classification: identify \(z_2\) as the positive among \(K-1\) negatives (InfoNCE, detailed in Appendix A):

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(z_1 \cdot z_2\, /\, \tau)}{\exp(z_1 \cdot z_2\, /\, \tau) \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \exp(z_1 \cdot z_k^-\, /\, \tau)} \]

where \(\tau\) is temperature and \(z_k^-\) are negatives.

SimCLR uses all other images in a large batch as negatives, requiring batch sizes of 4096 or more. CLIP replaces augmented views with image-text pairs: the positive for each image is its caption, and all other captions are negatives. CLIP sidesteps augmentation design entirely and produces representations that support zero-shot classification and cross-modal retrieval.

Contrastive methods are effective but require many negatives. Self-distillation, described next, achieves competitive results without them.

NoteB. Self-Distillation

Idea. Train two networks to agree on their output distributions, where one network is a slowly-updating copy of the other.

The setup has two networks with the same architecture: a student with parameters \(\theta\) and a teacher with parameters \(\xi\). The teacher is not updated by gradient descent. Instead, its weights are an exponential moving average of the student’s weights:

\[ \xi \;\leftarrow\; \tau\,\xi \;+\; (1-\tau)\,\theta \]

where \(\tau\) is a momentum coefficient close to 1 (e.g., 0.996). The teacher is always a slightly older, smoother version of the student.

Given two views \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), the student processes one view and the teacher the other. Both produce a distribution over \(K\) prototype dimensions via a softmax with temperature:

\[ p_s = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{f_\theta(x_1)}{\tau_s}\right), \qquad p_t = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{f_\xi(x_2) - c}{\tau_t}\right) \]

The student is trained to match the teacher’s output by minimizing the cross-entropy \(H(p_t,\, p_s)\), with no gradient flowing through the teacher.

Without countermeasures, this system collapses: both networks converge to the same constant output for all inputs. DINO prevents this with centering (subtract a running mean \(c\) from the teacher output to prevent single-dimension dominance) and sharpening (lower teacher temperature \(\tau_t < \tau_s\) to keep the training target peaked); see Appendix A for details.

BYOL established that negatives are not needed: it uses an asymmetric prediction head on the student side only, alongside the EMA teacher, to prevent collapse.

DINO applies this framework to ViTs, using the CLS token as representation and multi-crop training (teacher sees a global crop; student sees multiple smaller local crops). A striking finding: DINO attention heads spontaneously segment semantically coherent regions with no segmentation supervision, discussed further in Section 5.

NoteC. Masked Prediction

Idea. Hide part of the input and train the model to predict what is missing.

MAE (Masked Autoencoders) applies this to ViTs by masking a large fraction of patches (typically 75%) and reconstructing the original pixel values.

The encoder processes only visible patches, so its cost scales with \(N_\text{vis} = (1-m) \cdot N\) rather than the full sequence:

\[ \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times C}}{x} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{patch + mask}\;}\; \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}N_\text{vis}\times d}{X_\text{vis}} \;\xrightarrow{\;L\text{ blocks}\;}\; \underset{\rule{0pt}{1.5ex}N_\text{vis}\times d}{Z_\text{vis}} \]

A lightweight decoder then takes the encoder output alongside learnable mask tokens and reconstructs the pixel values of all \(N\) patches; the loss is MSE over the masked patches only.

The 75% mask ratio is critical. Low masking ratios allow reconstruction by interpolating from nearby visible patches, which does not require understanding object structure. A 75% mask forces the encoder to reason about global context to fill in large missing regions.

After pretraining, the decoder is discarded and the encoder is used as a feature extractor. MAE converges faster than DINO but produces somewhat weaker features for dense prediction tasks; DINOv2 combines both objectives to capture the benefits of each.

NoteWhy These Methods Work

The three methods enforce view consistency through different mechanisms, but the learned representations share two properties that go beyond what the training objective explicitly requires.

Object-centric structure. At no point during training does the model see segmentation labels, yet at convergence DINO’s CLS token attention maps highlight foreground objects and different heads attend to different semantic parts of the scene. This structure emerges from the requirement to match views of the same scene that differ in crop and appearance.

Geometry of the embedding space. Semantically related inputs cluster together, and representations spread uniformly across the hypersphere (see Appendix A for the alignment and uniformity decomposition).

Both properties have a direct consequence for transfer. SSL does not learn a classifier; it learns a representation space organized by semantic content. Fine-tuning on a labelled dataset finds boundaries within a structure that is already there. A linear probe works on many tasks because semantic consistency across views is what the training objective required, and semantic consistency is exactly what a linear classifier exploits.

All three methods optimize surrogate objectives (matching views, matching a teacher, reconstructing missing content), yet consistently recover semantic, object-centric representations that transfer across diverse downstream tasks. This is not a coincidence: each objective is satisfiable only by encoders that capture what is semantically stable about an image, not what differs between views.

NoteFine-Tuning

After SSL pretraining, the encoder is adapted to a specific task. Four strategies span the tradeoff between cost and performance. (See the ViT note for details on LoRA.)

Frozen encoder + linear probing. Only a linear classifier head is trained on top of the frozen backbone. This is the fastest option and a standard benchmark for representation quality: a good SSL representation should be linearly separable for most tasks.

Frozen encoder + nonlinear head. A small MLP head replaces the linear classifier, allowing more flexible adaptation without touching the backbone.

LoRA fine-tuning. The backbone is frozen; small low-rank update matrices are added to the attention projections and trained alongside the task head. This gives more flexibility than a fixed head while keeping trainable parameters below 1% of the backbone.

Full fine-tuning. All parameters are updated. Most effective when the target domain is distant from the pretraining distribution and when enough labelled data exists to avoid overfitting.

NotePractical Considerations

Augmentations. Standard natural-image augmentations (crops, color jitter, blur) often transfer to new domains without modification. For medical images, intensity normalization variants and orientation changes are worth adding; aggressive color jitter or grayscale may not apply depending on the modality.

Scale. SSL improves with both data scale and model scale. Diversity matters more than raw dataset size: a narrow distribution produces narrow representations. Among ViTs, ViT-L and larger consistently outperform ViT-B in downstream transfer when data allows.

Starting from a pretrained checkpoint. Training SSL from scratch is expensive and rarely necessary. Strong publicly available checkpoints (DINOv2, MAE-pretrained ViT-L) provide an excellent starting point for most domains.

NoteAppendix A: InfoNCE, Collapse, and Alignment vs. Uniformity
NoteInfoNCE

InfoNCE treats representation learning as a multi-class classification problem. Given a batch of \(N\) images, each with two augmented views, the loss over one positive pair \((z_i^{(1)}, z_i^{(2)})\) is:

\[ \mathcal{L}_i = -\log \frac{\exp\!\left(z_i^{(1)} \cdot z_i^{(2)}\,/\,\tau\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\!\left(z_i^{(1)} \cdot z_j^{(2)}\,/\,\tau\right)} \]

The \(j=i\) term in the denominator is the positive; all \(j \neq i\) terms are negatives drawn from the same batch. The denominator normalizes over all \(N\) candidates, so the model must score the true positive higher than every other image in the batch.

The temperature \(\tau\) controls sharpness. Low \(\tau\) concentrates gradients on the hardest negatives; high \(\tau\) distributes gradients more evenly. SimCLR uses \(\tau = 0.07\).

Minimizing InfoNCE is equivalent to maximizing a lower bound on the mutual information \(I(z^{(1)}; z^{(2)})\) between the two views.

NoteRepresentation Collapse

Representation collapse occurs when the encoder maps all inputs to the same point in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). A collapsed encoder minimizes any positive-pair similarity loss trivially, but its features carry no information.

Different methods prevent collapse in different ways.

Contrastive methods prevent collapse through negatives: a constant representation scores equally against all negatives, so it cannot minimize the InfoNCE denominator.

BYOL uses an asymmetric prediction head on the student side only. The asymmetry breaks the symmetry that would allow both networks to collapse simultaneously.

DINO uses two mechanisms: centering subtracts a running mean from the teacher output before softmax, preventing any single prototype dimension from dominating; sharpening applies a lower temperature to the teacher softmax, keeping the training target peaked rather than flat.

MAE prevents collapse by construction: pixel reconstruction has a non-trivial target that cannot be satisfied by a constant output.

NoteAlignment and Uniformity

SSL objectives can be decomposed into two complementary pressures.

Alignment measures how close positive pairs are:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\text{align} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,\, x^+) \sim p_\text{pos}} \left\|f(x) - f(x^+)\right\|^2 \]

Uniformity measures how evenly representations are spread across the unit hypersphere:

\[ \mathcal{L}_\text{uniform} = \log\; \mathbb{E}_{x,\,y \sim p_\text{data}} \left[\, e^{-2\|f(x) - f(y)\|^2} \,\right] \]

A good representation minimizes both: positives are close (alignment), and the overall distribution does not collapse to a subspace (uniformity). InfoNCE optimizes both simultaneously: the numerator promotes alignment, and the denominator promotes uniformity by penalizing any representation that is close to many others. Self-distillation methods primarily optimize alignment; their collapse-prevention mechanisms serve as a proxy for uniformity.
